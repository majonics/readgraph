# Vision

## The reader

The owner of this tool reads *around* a book as much as the book itself. Reading Dante's
*Divine Comedy* means wanting to know who Virgil really was, who Dante was, where he came
from, the Florence he was exiled from, the people he puts in Hell and why, and the
paintings, lectures and podcasts that bring all of it to life.

The problem is that this kind of curiosity has no floor. Florence leads to the Medici, the
Medici lead to the banking system, and three hours later the book is still closed.

## The idea

**readgraph gives that curiosity a shape and a limit.**

For a book the reader chooses (never "all books"), the tool builds a beautiful graph:

- the **book** at the centre,
- everything **in the book** around it,
- and exactly **one level of context** around those: enough to understand, never enough to get lost.

Every node is a doorway to the **best material available**, shown in the tool itself:
the Doré engraving, the Yale lecture, the *In Our Time* episode, the Stanford Encyclopedia entry.
The tool doesn't just list links. It presents the material.

The graph follows the reader's progress, so opening it at Canto V never spoils Canto XXXIII.

Once built, a graph stays. Over time the library becomes a personal atlas of everything the reader has
read and learned around, and entities like Florence or Aristotle connect across books.

## What it is

- A personal, local tool for one curious reader. It is not a social product or a platform.
- Two lenses: the **whole-book graph**, and a **focus view** for a single person or concept.
- AI-assisted *curation*: Claude researches, verifies and selects. The reader explores.

## What it is not

- Not a summary or book-notes app. It never replaces reading the book.
- Not an infinite wiki crawler. The depth limit is the feature.
- Not a link dump. Quality and presentation come first.

## Success looks like

- Opening a book's graph before a reading session and spending 10 good minutes, not 3 hours.
- Clicking "Virgil" and, within seconds, seeing who he was, why Dante chose him, a painting of him,
  and one lecture worth watching tonight.
- Never being spoiled.
- Wanting to show it to someone because it looks beautiful.
